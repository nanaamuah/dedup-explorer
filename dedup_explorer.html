10B observations. 1.11B jobs expected. About 1B counted.

If each job sits on about 3 sites and each site shows it about 3 times, 10 billion observations should hold 10B ÷ 9 ≈ 1.11 billion jobs. Canaria counts about 1 billion, so the average job is being seen about 10 times. The model below tests which explanations can close that gap.

10,000,000,000 observations ÷ (3 sites × 3 sightings) = 1,111,111,111 jobs counted ≈ 1,000,000,000 gap ≈ 111 million (10%)

What can close the gap

Averages multiply only when they are independent

The simple estimate assumes the number of sites and the number of sightings are unrelated. Jobs that are hard to fill likely stay up longer and get pushed to more boards, so the two counts rise together.

mean(sites × sightings) = mean(sites) × mean(sightings) + cov(sites, sightings)

A covariance of about 1 adds one observation per job, which is enough to turn 1.11B into 1.00B. In this model a correlation of 0.35 does it, and the top 10% of jobs then produce about 43% of all observations.

"About 3" is a wide number

3.2 sites × 3.1 sightings is 9.92 observations per job, which gives 1.01B. Both published totals also carry a "+", so a 10% gap sits inside the rounding of the inputs.

Deduplication can merge too much

If distinct openings get joined (twenty warehouse associate posts from one employer in one city, say), the unique count falls. Wrong merges on 10% of jobs would close the gap alone. Of the three, this one would hurt a customer, so I would check it first. The methodology keeps postings whose location cannot be tested as unique, which pushes the other way, so I expect this effect to be small.

What I would check in the real data

  1. Compute mean(sites × sightings) and mean(sites) × mean(sightings) over resolved jobs. The difference is the covariance; if it is near 1, the first explanation carries the gap.
  2. Draw 200 duplicate groups, stratified by group size, and check them by hand for false merges. Large groups come first, because one bad merge there removes many jobs.
  3. Plot observations per job on a log scale and split the tail by employer. Always-hiring postings from large employers and staffing firms would show up as a long right tail.

One more thing I noticed

The methodology page says the 1B+ figure is unique by URL and source job key, and that grouping the same job across boards happens in a second stage. If that is the 1B in the question, the same job on three sites is still three of those postings.

The fair comparison is then 10B ÷ 3 ≈ 3.3B postings expected against 1B counted, so each URL would be seen about 10 times, and the number of cross-board unique jobs would sit below 1B. One line saying which of the two counts the 1B refers to would settle it for a buyer.

Model notes

Sites = 1 + Poisson((s − 1) · eσz − σ²/2) and sightings = 1 + Poisson((v − 1) · eσz′ − σ²/2), where s and v are the averages you set and z′ is correlated with z at r. Observations per job is sites × sightings, and its expected value has a closed form:

E[obs per job] = s + v − 1 + (s − 1)(v − 1) · eσ²r

The headline numbers and the bridge use that formula. The distribution chart and the top-10% share come from 60,000 simulated jobs with a fixed seed. I checked the formula against 2 million simulated jobs at the default settings: 10.004 observations per job from the formula and 10.009 from the simulation.

Reproduce it in R:

set.seed(1)
n     <- 1e6
sigma <- 0.8   # how uneven jobs are
rho   <- 0.35  # sites and sightings move together

z     <- rnorm(n)
zv    <- rho * z + sqrt(1 - rho^2) * rnorm(n)
sites <- 1 + rpois(n, 2 * exp(sigma * z  - sigma^2 / 2))
views <- 1 + rpois(n, 2 * exp(sigma * zv - sigma^2 / 2))
obs   <- sites * views

c(sites = mean(sites), views = mean(views),
  obs = mean(obs), cov = cov(sites, views))
10e9 / mean(obs)   # unique jobs implied by 10B observations
